## 1. The Turtle

In Terry Pratchett's Discworld, the world is a flat disc resting on the backs of four
elephants, who stand on the shell of the Great A'Tuin, a turtle swimming through
space. Nobody in the Discworld asks what the turtle stands on. The question is not
forbidden; it simply has no answer inside the world, and the world works perfectly
well without one.

That is the image this document starts from. **Every paradigm rests on something it
does not demonstrate** — axioms, criteria, founding assumptions. A scientific theory
has its turtle, a mythology has its turtle, a child's classification of animals has
its turtle. What differs between them is not *whether* they have one, but whether
they show it. Most paradigms keep their turtle out of sight, below the disc, where it
is easy to forget. The Discworld is the rare world that puts its turtle in plain
view — which is why it serves here as the anchor for **internal coherence**: a
paradigm is coherent *on its turtle*, and its coherence can only be judged once the
turtle is declared.

Two consequences follow, and the rest of the document unfolds them.

- A paradigm is never judged in the absolute; it is judged **on its own criteria**
  first (Section 3), and only then compared with others.
- Since no paradigm stands on nothing, the useful question is not "which one is
  true?" but **"which one am I standing in, and can I step into another?"**
  (Section 4).

## 2. "Truth" and "Reality"

Around 375 BC, in Book VII of the *Republic*, Plato describes prisoners chained in a
cave, facing a wall. Behind them a fire casts the shadows of objects carried past;
the prisoners, who have never seen anything else, take the shadows for the real
things. One prisoner is freed, turns around, and discovers that what everyone called
"reality" was a projection.

TSCG keeps the first lesson of the cave and declines the second.

- **The first lesson** — what a community calls *reality* may be the shadow cast by
  its own setting — is the founding move of TSCG's Map–Territory distinction
  (`TerritoryMap_Dichotomy.md`; Korzybski's *the map is not the territory*).
- **The second lesson** — that there is, outside the cave, a single true world of
  Forms that the philosopher can reach — is the part TSCG does not adopt. Once every
  paradigm has its turtle, there is no privileged outside from which all caves can be
  ranked. There are other caves, other fires, other walls; some cast sharper shadows
  than others, for some purposes.

For the record, the same refusal has a documented counterpart in second-order
cybernetics — the cybernetics of *observing* systems rather than observed ones.
Humberto Maturana wrote that *anything said is said by an observer* (1970), and later
proposed to put *objectivity in parenthesis*: not to deny an observer-independent
reality, but to decline to invoke it to settle a disagreement (1988); Heinz von
Foerster called the programme the *cybernetics of cybernetics* (1974). TSCG cites these
as kin, not as a foundation. The radical-constructivist reading some draw from them —
that reality is constructed by the observer (Ernst von Glasersfeld) — is itself a
paradigm with its own turtle, and TSCG does not adopt it: it keeps a Territory from
which every Map departs (δ₁).

The words *Truth* and *Reality*, used without qualification, claim exactly that
privileged outside. That is why this document treats them as **usurped** when they
are used bare — not because nothing is true or real, but because the bare word hides
the perspective it speaks from.

What replaces them is a working rule, the one every other principle in this document
depends on:

> **Always declare which perspective — which paradigm, which criteria — you are
> speaking from.**

This is not relativism in the sense of "anything goes". Perspectives are
**epistemological tools**: each makes some things visible and hides others, each has
its biases and its domain of use, and some are better tools than others for a given
job. George Box put the same idea in one line: *all models are wrong, but some are
useful*. A toolkit that teaches modelling owes its users exactly this — the habit of
naming the tool they are holding, and of knowing what it cannot do.

## 3. Coherence Relative to Criteria

### 3.1 The child and the whale

A child who says that a whale is a fish is not being incoherent. The child is applying
a criterion — *an animal that lives entirely in the water* — and on that criterion the
classification is correct. The whale lives in the sea, swims, has fins and no legs.
The child's paradigm is coherent on its turtle.

The verdict changes only when the criterion changes. On the criteria of comparative
anatomy and physiology — lungs, warm blood, live birth, milk — the whale is a mammal,
as Linnaeus placed it in 1758 (and as Aristotle had already observed, noting that
dolphins breathe air and suckle their young). Change the criterion once more, to the
strict genealogical criterion of cladistics, and the verdict turns again: "fish" is
then not a group that excludes the land vertebrates at all — whales, like humans, sit
*inside* the lobe-finned fishes, as their descendants. Three criteria, three coherent
answers. None of them is incoherent; each is coherent on its own turtle.

### 3.2 A courtroom case

The question was once settled by a jury. In 1818, in New York, the case *Maurice v.
Judd* turned on whether whale oil was "fish oil" under a state law requiring fish oil
to be inspected. A naturalist, Samuel Latham Mitchill, testified that the whale is a
mammal. The jury found that the whale is a fish — on the criterion that mattered to
the merchants, the sailors and the law: what lives and is caught in the sea. The
historian D. Graham Burnett tells the story in *Trying Leviathan* (2007). It was not
ignorance against knowledge; it was **two coherent systems of classification**, each
with its own criteria and its own purposes, meeting in one room.

### 3.3 Dreams, worlds, and the Discworld

The same holds well beyond classification. A dream builds a world that feels entirely
coherent while one is inside it. A novel builds a world that holds together on its own
rules — the Discworld most visibly, with its magic, its physics and its turtle. A
mythology orders the cosmos on its own premises. Each is coherent **relative to its
criteria**; none can be judged by importing the criteria of another without first
saying so.

### 3.4 What an "error" is, then

Seen this way, an error is not an absence of coherence. It is a **mismatch between
criteria**: a statement coherent on one turtle, read on another. Two things follow.

- **The error has a residue.** Every criterion leaves something out. The habitat
  criterion leaves out the whale coming up to breathe — a detail that does not fit, and
  that is exactly the *epistemic residue* of that criterion
  (`The_Epistemic_Residue.md`). The residue is where a switch of criteria begins.
- **The error is not something to mock.** Treating a different criterion as stupidity
  is the cheapest way of not seeing it. The productive move is to ask *which criterion
  makes this coherent?* — and then to decide, explicitly, which criterion serves the
  purpose at hand.
- **The error is knowledge.** A path that turned out wrong tells the next person where
  not to go, and often why. Yet research rarely writes its dead ends down: results
  that "did not work" stay in the drawer — what the psychologist Robert Rosenthal named
  the *file drawer problem* (1979). The cost is double: others walk the same dead end
  again, and the published record looks more certain than the work behind it was.
  Journals of negative results and pre-registered studies are attempts to empty the
  drawer.

TSCG tries to keep its own drawer open. Its documents record the designs they
abandoned and why: the axes rejected by the facet decision record, the analogies
refused in `The_Epistemic_Residue.md`, the design-history table of
`ModelSupersession.md`. This document adds one more: a facet called `RealityCheck`,
proposed to label the *kind* of paradigm a maquette stands in, then withdrawn in
favour of declared criteria (Section 3.5) — because a label for the kind classifies
without explaining, and quietly ranks.

### 3.5 Declared criteria

For a maquette, the consequence is a single requirement: **declare the criteria** —
the turtle — the maquette is built on. Not a label for the *kind* of paradigm (science,
myth, fiction…), which classifies without explaining, but the criteria themselves,
which say what makes the maquette coherent and therefore where its residue will lie.

TSCG already has an embryo of this. Several M0 instances carry an `observer` field —
"Ecologist / Systems Biologist", "Broadcast Engineer / Video Technician", "Human
(musicological/systemic perspective)". It declares *who* is looking; the next step is
to declare *by which criteria*.

## 4. Context Switching

### 4.1 The principle

A **context** is a paradigm together with its criteria: a formalism, a scale, a
discipline, a perspective. **Context switching** is moving the same object of study
from one context to another — not to find out which context is the true one, but to
see what the object looks like there, and what can be *done* with it there.

The name comes from computing, where it fits closely: a processor switches context by
saving the state of one task and loading another. Nothing is lost, and the switch can
be made back. That is the intended sense — switching is symmetrical; no context is the
"home" one, and no context is a parenthesis inside another.

### 4.2 New handles

The payoff of a switch is a set of **new handles** on the object: operations that were
impossible, or clumsy, in the first context and become easy in the second. The history
of mathematics is a long record of it.

- **Descartes** (*La Géométrie*, 1637) switched geometry into algebra: curves became
  equations, and problems of construction became problems of calculation.
- **Galois** switched the solving of polynomial equations into the study of their
  symmetries: whether an equation can be solved by radicals became a property of a
  group.
- **Fourier** (*Théorie analytique de la chaleur*, 1822) switched functions into sums
  of oscillations; the transform that carries his name switches a signal from time to
  frequency, where a convolution, laborious in one context, becomes a simple
  multiplication in the other.
- **Wiles** proved Fermat's Last Theorem (1995) by switching it into the context of
  elliptic curves and modular forms — a context that, for three centuries, had seemed
  to have nothing to do with it.

### 4.3 Serendipity

The switch is often where discovery happens unannounced. Henri Poincaré recounts that,
after weeks of work on what he called Fuchsian functions, the idea came to him as he
set foot on an omnibus during an excursion: the transformations he had been using were
the same as those of non-Euclidean geometry. He had not been thinking about it at all.
Jacques Hadamard collected such accounts in *The Psychology of Invention in the
Mathematical Field* (1945).

Two authors turned this into method or theory.

- **George Pólya** (*How to Solve It*, 1945) made it an explicit heuristic: *can you
  restate the problem? Can you solve a related problem? Change the representation.*
- **Arthur Koestler** (*The Act of Creation*, 1964) named it **bisociation**:
  perceiving one situation in two frames of reference, each coherent in itself but
  habitually incompatible with the other. He saw it as the common mechanism of
  scientific discovery, of art — and of the joke, whose punchline is a switch of
  context the listener did not see coming.

### 4.4 What switching is not

Switching is not dissolving every context into one, and it is not ranking them. The
point of moving a problem from geometry to algebra is not to declare geometry false;
Descartes did not abolish figures. The point is to **hold two coherent contexts and
move between them**, keeping in each what it does best. A switch that preserves the
structure of the object is a powerful tool; a switch that only projects a structure
onto it is a trap (Section 11). Knowing which is which is part of the discipline.

## 5. A Worked Thread: How Many Infinities?

The infinite is a good place to watch context switching at work, because each switch
changed not only the answer but the question — and because, at the end, the turtle
shows itself.

### 5.1 One infinity

In everyday use there is one infinity: *the endless*, what has no last term. It is a
perfectly serviceable context. Counting never stops; space goes on; that is all one
needs to say.

### 5.2 Two infinities

The calculus of Newton and Leibniz (late seventeenth century) added a second one: the
**infinitely small**, alongside the infinitely large. Infinitesimals gave mathematics
handles it had never had — on speed at an instant, on the area under a curve. They
were also attacked: Bishop Berkeley mocked them in *The Analyst* (1734) as "the ghosts
of departed quantities". In the nineteenth century Cauchy and Weierstrass removed them,
replacing them with limits — a switch of context that kept the handles and dropped the
contested objects. In the 1960s Abraham Robinson brought infinitesimals back, on a
rigorous footing, as non-standard analysis. Seen through `ModelSupersession.md`, the
infinitesimal was set aside, then restored.

### 5.3 Infinitely many infinities

Galileo had already noticed something strange (*Two New Sciences*, 1638): the perfect
squares 1, 4, 9, 16… are fewer than the whole numbers — most numbers are not squares —
yet they can be paired off one to one with them (1↔1, 2↔4, 3↔9…). On the criterion
*the part is smaller than the whole*, there are fewer squares; on the criterion *two
collections are equal if they can be paired off exactly*, there are as many. Galileo
concluded that "greater", "equal" and "smaller" do not apply to infinite quantities.

Georg Cantor, two and a half centuries later, made the opposite choice of criterion:
he kept one-to-one pairing as the definition of "as many". That single switch opened a
new world. With it, Cantor showed that there are **more** real numbers than whole
numbers, and that for any infinite collection there is a larger one — an unending
ladder of infinities, the alephs ℵ₀, ℵ₁, ℵ₂… The same disagreement as the child and the
whale, two criteria and two coherent answers, here produced one of the most fertile
theories of modern mathematics.

### 5.4 The turtle, in plain view

Cantor asked a natural question: is there an infinity strictly between that of the
whole numbers and that of the real numbers? This is the **continuum hypothesis**, the
first problem on Hilbert's famous list of 1900. The answer, when it came, was not yes
and not no. Gödel (1940) showed that the hypothesis cannot be refuted from the standard
axioms of set theory; Cohen (1963) showed that it cannot be proved from them either.
It is **independent**: true in some coherent mathematical worlds, false in others,
depending on which axioms one adds.

Here mathematics does what the Discworld does: it shows its turtle. The answer to a
precise question depends, literally, on the axioms one stands on. There is no
context-free fact of the matter to be found by looking harder — only the choice of a
context, and the obligation to declare it.

### 5.5 Infinity in the palm of the hand

The thread ends where Blake's quatrain begins. The segment from 0 to 1 is bounded — one
can hold it in the hand — yet on Cantor's criterion it contains **as many points as the
whole infinite line**. On the criterion of length, it measures 1; on the criterion of
counting points, it is as large as everything. Helge von Koch's snowflake (1904) does the
same with shape: its outline is infinitely long, yet it encloses a finite area. A
limited space that contains the infinitely small, a world in a grain of sand: each is a
switch of criterion, made visible.

## 6. Perspectives

### 6.1 The tick's world

In 1934 the biologist Jakob von Uexküll set out to describe animals' worlds *as they
are for the animal*. His most famous case is the tick. Of everything around it, the
tick registers three signals: the smell of butyric acid given off by mammals' skin, the
warmth of a body, the touch of hair. That is its world — Uexküll's word is *Umwelt* —
and it is complete: the tick lacks nothing it can miss, and its behaviour is perfectly
coherent within it.

The *Umwelt* is context switching applied to living beings. Each perspective is a
context, with its own signals, its own goals, its own criteria of what matters.

### 6.2 Being a bee, a liver cell, a mitochondrion, an ant colony

These are exactly the perspectives a maquette can make accessible — experiences that
human nature does not offer directly.

- **A bee** sees ultraviolet patterns on flowers that we cannot see, navigates by the
  polarisation of light, and tells its sisters where the food is by dancing — as Karl
  von Frisch showed, work that earned him a share of the Nobel prize in 1973.
- **A liver cell** lives in a world of chemical messages — insulin, glucagon — and
  answers by storing or releasing sugar for a body it will never see.
- **A mitochondrion** lives by a gradient: protons pumped across a membrane and allowed
  back through a turbine-like enzyme, as Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory (1961)
  described.
- **An ant colony** is a perspective no single ant holds: the colony "decides" where to
  forage while each ant follows local traces — a switch of scale from the individual to
  the collective, Blake's world in a grain of sand read the other way round.

In TSCG terms, each such perspective is a maquette waiting to be built — and it needs
all three grammars, not only the first. A perspective is above all a *way of knowing*,
so the Map and Stereopsis sides carry most of it.

| Grammar | Primitives | What it asks of a perspective | The tick, the bee |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Territory** (Gt, `×`) | `A` `St` `F` `It` `D` | What is the observer as a system: what it tends toward, what holds it together, what flows through it, what information it carries, how it changes? | The tick waits, then drops; the bee forages and returns |
| **Map** (Gm, `+`) | `R` `E` `V` `O` `Im` | How does it know: what can it **observe** (`O`), how does it **represent** it (`R`), can it **check** (`V`), can its knowing **evolve** (`E`), can it be **shared** with others (`Im`)? | The tick observes three signals and nothing else; the bee represents a flower's position and shares it by dancing |
| **Stereopsis** (Gs, `\|`) | `T` `K` `Ss` `L` (+ poles) | At what **time scale** (`T`) does it live; what does it **know** (`K`); through which **symbols** (`Ss`); toward what does it **converge**, and can it tell it is getting closer (`L`) — an attractor in a space of states, not necessarily a place? | The dance is a symbol; the colony lives on a longer time scale than any bee; the tick converges on warmth, the bee on the hive |

An *Umwelt* maquette is one of the most direct ways a newcomer can experience a context
switch rather than read about one — and a good way to see that the Map side, how a world
is *known*, is where perspectives differ most.

### 6.3 Willing suspension of disbelief

To enter such a perspective one has to accept, for a while, its criteria as one's own.
Samuel Taylor Coleridge named the move in 1817: *that willing suspension of disbelief
for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith* (*Biographia Literaria*). Fiction relies
on it; so does every serious attempt to understand a paradigm from the inside — a
historian reading Ptolemy, an anthropologist inside a cosmology, a newcomer inside the
Discworld.

Two words in Coleridge's phrase carry the weight. *Willing*: the suspension is chosen.
*For the moment*: it is held for a time, and can be lifted. That is what makes it a
**switch** and not a conversion (Section 7).

### 6.4 The limit: structure, not experience

TSCG already uses the image of the bat — it does not see the cave; it hears the cave's
echo (`TerritoryMap_Dichotomy.md`). The philosopher Thomas Nagel used the same animal to
draw a limit (*What Is It Like to Be a Bat?*, 1974): we can know a great deal about how
a bat's echolocation works, and still not know *what it is like* to be one.

The limit applies to every maquette in this section. A maquette of the bee, the cell or
the colony can reconstruct the **structure** of a perspective — its signals, its
criteria, its goals, its flows. It does not deliver the **experience** of it. That is
not a failure of the maquette; it is what a maquette is, and saying so is part of
declaring its criteria.

## 7. Locked in One Context

### 7.1 Coherence does not tell them apart

If every paradigm is coherent on its turtle, then coherence cannot be what separates a
fertile perspective from one that closes in on itself. A dream is coherent while it
lasts. A delusional belief can be highly systematic — clinicians speak of a
*systematised* delusion. A dogma is often admirably consistent. Something else must
make the difference.

The proposal of this document is that the difference lies in **the ability to switch**.
A context one can leave and re-enter is a tool. A context one can no longer leave has
stopped being a tool and become an enclosure.

### 7.2 Dreaming, and dreaming lucidly

In an ordinary dream, the dreamer is wholly inside the dream's world and does not know
it is a dream. In a **lucid** dream the world may be exactly the same, but the dreamer
knows it is a dream — and can sometimes act on that knowledge. Lucid dreaming has been
studied in sleep laboratories since the 1980s, where dreamers signalled their lucidity
with agreed eye movements.

The contrast is a clean image of what this document means by switching. The lucid
dreamer has not left the dream; they hold *two* contexts at once — the dream, and the
knowledge that it is one. In TSCG's vocabulary this is **stereopsis**: two eyes, Map and
Territory, and the gap δ₁ between them kept visible.

### 7.3 Delusion — a structure, not a verdict on persons

What clinical psychiatry calls a delusion is not defined by strange content — its
content can be coherent, and sometimes partly true — but by being **held fixed against
everything that might revise it**. Paranoia, in this sense, is a perspective with its
own internal coherence and no remaining switch.

Two cautions belong here. First, this document offers no clinical knowledge and makes
no diagnosis; it borrows a structure to understand a structure. Second, the enclosure
it describes is not a property of "other people". Everyone holds some beliefs fixed
against evidence; confirmation bias is the ordinary form of it. As
`The_Epistemic_Residue.md` puts it about its own map of failure modes: *no corner is
"other people."*

### 7.4 Dogma — the collective enclosure

A dogma is the same structure held by a community: a consensus that has immunised
itself against anything that could revise it — Popper's *immunizing stratagem*,
Lakatos's *degenerating research programme*. `ModelSupersession.md` (§4.6) keeps the
word for exactly this pathology, and refuses to use it as a label for a community.
What makes a consensus a dogma is not that it is wrong — it may well be right — but that
it is no longer held in a way that allows a switch. Section 8 returns to this from the
side of the individual: a true statement recited is held the same way as a dogma.

### 7.5 Immersion is not the problem

None of this makes immersion suspect. Working inside one paradigm for a long time is
how most good work gets done; Kuhn called it *normal science*, and it is productive
precisely because it does not keep questioning its turtle. A mathematician may spend
years inside one formalism; a novelist inside one world.

The problem is not staying in a context. It is **losing the ability to leave it** —
forgetting that it is a context at all. That is why, for a maquette, declaring its
criteria is not bureaucracy: it is the saved state that keeps the switch available.

## 8. Rediscovery over Recitation

### 8.1 Knowledge tied down

In Plato's *Meno*, Socrates draws a square in the sand and asks a slave boy, who has
never studied geometry, how to build a square of double the area. The boy guesses wrong,
twice; then, by questioning alone, he finds the answer himself — the square built on
the diagonal. Later in the dialogue Socrates draws the lesson: a true opinion is like
the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are tied down. What ties an opinion
down, and turns it into knowledge, is working out the reason for it.

The distinction cuts across the one between true and false:

| | Worked out for oneself | Recited |
|---|---|---|
| **True belief** | knowledge, tied down | a true statement held like a dogma |
| **False belief** | an error that can be found and corrected | a false dogma |

The top-right cell is the uncomfortable one. "The Earth is round", repeated without any
idea how one would know it, is true — and held exactly as a dogma is held.

### 8.2 *Nullius in verba*

The Royal Society of London took as its motto, in the 1660s, *nullius in verba*: take
nobody's word for it. Not *believe nothing*, but *do not let a statement stand on
authority alone* — check what can be checked.

### 8.3 Those who checked

This is where people who set out to test a "settled" fact deserve more than mockery.
Some people drawn to flat-Earth ideas are less committed to a flat Earth than to a
question: *how would I know, myself, that it is round?* Some of them have gone and
looked — and come back saying they had been wrong. Having checked, and accepted the
result, they hold the roundness of the Earth more firmly than most of those who laughed
at them. In the terms of the table above, they crossed from the bottom-left cell to the
top-left one, the only crossing that turns belief into knowledge.

The value lies in accepting the verdict of one's own test. The documentary *Behind the
Curve* (2018) shows the other outcome: an experiment designed by flat-Earth proponents
detects the Earth's rotation, and the result is set aside. Checking is worth something
only when the check is allowed to win — which is the switch of Section 7, made on
oneself.

### 8.4 Theorems and openings

The same holds for what is learned rather than believed. A theorem memorised is a
statement one can use; a theorem rediscovered — even clumsily, even with help — is a
statement one can *rebuild*, adapt and recognise in another context. Chess players
know the difference between an opening memorised move by move and one whose ideas are
understood: the first is lost at the first unexpected reply. Richard Feynman left on his
blackboard, at his death in 1988, a line that says it shortly: *What I cannot create, I
do not understand.*

### 8.5 Limits

No one can rediscover everything. Most of what anyone knows rests on the testimony of
others, and trusting competent testimony is rational, not lazy — the philosopher John
Hardwig called this *epistemic dependence* (1985). What has value is not the impossible
duty of checking everything, but the **capacity** to check, and the habit of checking
*some* things for oneself — enough to know what checking is like, and what one is taking
on trust. Rediscovery also produces errors along the way; by Section 3.4, those are part
of the yield, not waste.

### 8.6 What a maquette is for

This is, finally, what a TSCG maquette is for. Building one is not reciting what a fire
or a cell or a market is; it is rediscovering it — choosing the criteria, finding the
pieces, seeing where the residue falls. The User Guide already says so in its own way:
its exercises ship with a worked example, labelled a spoiler, and the advice is to do
the exercise first. The toolkit's pedagogical vocation and the epistemology of this
document are the same commitment, seen from two sides.

## 9. Ideas as Living Systems — a Candidate Maquette

Ideas, models and theories are often described as if they were alive: they are born,
compete, mutate, spread, defend themselves, and die. The comparison has a long record.

- **Karl Popper**'s evolutionary epistemology (*Objective Knowledge*, 1972) treats
  conjectures as variants and criticism as selection — the point of a theory being,
  in his phrase, that it can die in our stead.
- **Stephen Toulmin** (*Human Understanding*, 1972) described the evolution of concepts
  within scientific disciplines.
- **Richard Dawkins** (*The Selfish Gene*, 1976) coined the *meme* — a unit of cultural
  transmission, copied, varied and selected.
- **David Hull** (*Science as a Process*, 1988) worked out the selection of theories in
  the social life of science.

`ModelSupersession.md` already speaks this language without saying so: a refutation
looks like an extinction, a bounding like a retreat to a niche, a restoration like a
recolonisation. Lakatos's *protective belt* around a hard core reads naturally as a
homeostasis.

This document does **not** adopt the comparison as a principle. It proposes it as a
**candidate maquette** — and, like every maquette, as one that must be able to fail.
Memetics has long been criticised for never identifying a clear *unit of replication*
comparable to the gene; the analogy may be decorative. The TSCG test is direct: build
the maquette *a theory as a living system*, on all three grammars, and see whether the
primitives hold without forcing.

- **Territory** (Gt): what the theory tends toward (`A`), what holds it together (`St`),
  what flows through it (`F`: citations, teaching, translations), what it carries
  (`It`), how it changes (`D`: variation and selection).
- **Map** (Gm): how it is represented (`R`: its formalism), whether it can be checked
  (`V`), what consequences it makes observable (`O`), how far it can evolve without
  breaking (`E`: Lakatos's protective belt), whether it connects with other theories
  (`Im`: what Whewell called consilience).
- **Stereopsis** (Gs): its lifetime (`T`), its notation (`Ss`), and whether its
  successive versions converge on what it aims at (`L`) — convergence toward an
  attractor in a space of states, judged ordinally, closer or farther (the M3
  definition of `L`; not a place, not a domain of validity).

If the primitives hold, the comparison is a structure worth having; if they have to be
forced, it is a metaphor, and the maquette says so. Either verdict is useful.

## 10. What TSCG Already Does

Context switching is not a new feature to add to TSCG. It names what the toolkit
already does.

- **Transdisciplinarity** is switching one system across disciplines — reading a
  feedback loop in a cell, a market and a circuit.
- **The three grammars** — Territory (`×`, Gt), Map (`+`, Gm), Stereopsis (`|`, Gs) —
  are three formalisms applied to the same object.
- **Stereopsis** is holding two contexts at once, with the gap δ₁ kept visible — the
  lucid dreamer's stance (Section 7.2).
- **`m3:Functor`**, in the category-theoretic vocabulary of M3, is the name for a
  switch that preserves structure.
- **The `observer` field** carried by many M0 instances is a first declaration of
  perspective (Section 3.5).
- **`ModelSupersession.md`** describes switches made by whole communities, recorded by
  history (Section 12).
- **The maquette itself** is a willing suspension of disbelief with declared criteria —
  a switch into a context, made in order to rediscover it.

What this document adds is the principle that ties these together, and the rule that
follows: always declare the context.

## 11. Guard-Rails

**Not every switch preserves everything.** Moving an object into another context keeps
some of its structure and drops some. What the new context cannot absorb is a residue
(`The_Epistemic_Residue.md`). Between some paradigms, Kuhn argued, translation is only
ever partial — the same word, *mass* or *species*, does not mean the same thing on both
sides. A switch should say what it loses.

**A switch can manufacture a correspondence.** The productive switch *reveals* a
structure the object already had in both contexts; the deceptive one *projects* a
structure onto it. `The_Epistemic_Residue.md` names this failure the synesthetic
corner — bindings hallucinated across contexts — and shows the discipline at work by
rejecting three of its own tempting analogies. Every switch proposed in this document,
including Section 9, is held to the same test.

**Declaring criteria is not "anything goes".** That every paradigm stands on a turtle
does not make all paradigms equally good for every purpose. Some contexts give better
handles on a given problem than others; the choice of context is judged by what it lets
one do. What the principle rules out is not judgment, but judgment that hides the
criteria it uses.

**No new pieces in the kit.** This document adds no M2 concept and no ontology property.
If "declared criteria" ever becomes something an M0 instance carries formally, it will
go through the ontology-diagnosis pipeline and the facet legitimacy test — the test that
`RealityCheck` would have had to pass (Section 3.4).

## 13. The Shore

> *"As our island of knowledge grows, so does the shore of our ignorance."*
> — John Archibald Wheeler

**How to declare criteria.** The principle is stated; its form is not. Should an M0
instance extend its `observer` field into a declaration of criteria, carry a short text
in its README, or something else? Undecided — and to be paid for by use before anything
is graved.

**What counts as one context.** This document groups formalisms, scales, disciplines and
perspectives under one word. Whether they behave alike under switching, or need to be
told apart, is open.

**What a switch preserves.** Mathematics can say precisely when a switch preserves
structure (a functor, an isomorphism). Outside mathematics there is no such criterion
yet, and incommensurability suggests there may never be a complete one.

**Comparing contexts without a privileged outside.** If no context is "the real one",
contexts are compared by what they let one do. How that judgment is made, and by whom,
is the question `MultisubjectiveScoreEvaluationProtocol.md` begins to answer for scores;
for contexts it remains open.

**The clinical side.** Section 7 borrows a structure from clinical psychiatry and claims
nothing about it. Whether the switching view has anything to offer there is not this
toolkit's question.

**Maquettes not yet built.** The *Umwelt* exercise (Section 6.2) and the living-theory
maquette (Section 9) are proposed, not done.

**Downstream revisions.** `ModelSupersession.md` is to be revised to rest on this
document: the postulate moves here, `RealityCheck` gives way to declared criteria, and
its four relations are re-read as switches kept open, partly open, closed or re-opened.

This document, too, is held until further notice.
